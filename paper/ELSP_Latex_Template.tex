\noindent\textbf{\textbf{\textcolor[RGB]{0,131,255}{Abstract:}}} Vision-language-action (VLA) policies can degrade when the deployment view provides insufficient task-relevant evidence for action prediction.
Existing approaches typically improve viewpoint robustness by synthesizing novel views or learning 3D representations that preserve scene structure across viewpoints. However, they do not explicitly determine which visual evidence a weak-view policy needs for each action prediction. We introduce \textbf{Action-Conditioned Privileged Distillation (ACPD)}, which uses a frozen privileged-view teacher to supervise a weak-view student. ACPD uses the teacher's attention weights to extract visual cues for each future control step and trains the student to predict and use these cues for action generation. An action correlation loss aligns the relative variation of teacher and student flow-velocity predictions. At deployment, the student uses a single camera view and operates without the teacher.
We evaluate ACPD with $\pi_{0.5}$ using synchronized multi-view demonstrations from four LIBERO suites. ACPD improves over single-view fine-tuning across all four deployment views, raising the overall mean success rate from 71.09\% to 74.78\%. In the back-view setting, ACPD achieves 61.05\% success, compared with 52.40\% for a multi-view-to-single-view knowledge distillation baseline.

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies map language instructions and visual observations to robot actions.
Diffusion and flow-matching action generators have been widely adopted in VLA policies~\cite{chi2025diffusionpolicy,wen2025diffusionvla,black2025pi0}.
However, their performance is constrained by the visual evidence available at deployment.
When object pose, spatial relations, or gripper-object contact are occluded or poorly visible, a policy can perform substantially worse than one using a more informative view.
On LIBERO, policies trained and evaluated separately for each camera view exhibit substantial differences in task success (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).
Workspace geometry and robot hardware can constrain camera placement, leaving a less informative deployment view.
This motivates weak-view policy learning, where a policy uses a limited view at deployment but can receive training-time supervision from a more informative view.

View-diversity methods address viewpoint sensitivity using synchronized multi-camera demonstrations or synthesized novel views~\cite{ablett2021multiview,tian2025vista}. Auxiliary views also support visual representation learning~\cite{seo2023multiview}, while geometry-aware methods encode scenes using explicit 3D representations~\cite{shridhar2023peract,goyal2023rvt}. These strategies improve viewpoint robustness but do not explicitly identify the visual evidence needed for each action prediction under weak-view deployment.

Privileged distillation supervises a weak-view policy using a teacher with more informative views during training. Prior work transfers knowledge from a multi-camera teacher to a single-camera policy by matching teacher actions and visual features~\cite{acar2024multicamera}. However, cross-view supervision faces a correspondence problem: relevant evidence may be occluded in the weak view, while shared scene elements can appear at different image locations and token positions. Consequently, matching complete representations across views can impose poorly aligned targets on the student. Effective cross-view supervision must identify teacher information useful for each future action, since different actions may require different visual evidence.

To address these challenges, we propose \textbf{Action-Conditioned Privileged Distillation (ACPD)}, a cross-view distillation framework that transfers action-conditioned visual cues from a privileged-view teacher to a weak-view student.
ACPD trains the student alongside a frozen teacher using synchronized observations with the same instruction, robot state, and action target. The teacher receives informative camera views, while the student receives a single weak view. For each future control step, ACPD uses attention weights from the teacher's action expert to aggregate visual features from each privileged view into a compact cue. The student learns to predict these cues from its own action representations and incorporates them into action generation. These cues provide supervision without requiring visual-token alignment across views.
At the action level, ACPD introduces an \textit{action correlation loss} (ACL) that uses Pearson correlation to align the relative variation of teacher and student flow-velocity predictions. The ground-truth flow-matching objective supervises the student's absolute velocity values. At deployment, the student uses a single camera view without the teacher.

We evaluate ACPD with $\pi_{0.5}$ across four LIBERO suites and four single-view deployment settings. ACPD improves mean success over single-view fine-tuning in all four views, with gains of 2.90--4.00 percentage points and an overall increase from 71.09\% to 74.78\%. In the back-view setting, ACPD achieves 61.05\% success, exceeding the evaluated multi-view-to-single-view distillation baseline by 8.65 percentage points.


Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce ACPD, a cross-view distillation framework that trains a weak-view student to predict and use action-conditioned visual cues extracted from privileged views using the teacher's attention weights.
    \item We propose an action correlation loss that uses Pearson correlation to align the relative variation of teacher and student flow-velocity predictions, complementing ground-truth flow-matching supervision.
    \item Experiments across four LIBERO suites demonstrate gains over single-view fine-tuning in all four deployment views. In the back-view setting, ACPD also outperforms a multi-view-to-single-view distillation baseline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Generative VLA Policies}

Large-scale datasets such as BridgeData V2, Open X-Embodiment, and DROID support generalist robot learning~\cite{walke2023bridgev2,oneill2024oxe,khazatsky2024droid}. RT-1 learns language-conditioned control from visual observations, while RT-2 adapts pretrained vision-language models for action prediction~\cite{brohan2023rt1,zitkovich2023rt2}. Octo and OpenVLA are pretrained on heterogeneous robot data for adaptation across robot embodiments~\cite{ghosh2024octo,kim2025openvla}.

Beyond data and pretraining, VLA policies also differ in how they represent and generate action sequences. Action chunking predicts sequences of future actions~\cite{zhao2023act}. FAST encodes action chunks as discrete tokens, while OpenVLA-OFT predicts continuous action chunks in parallel~\cite{pertsch2025fast,kim2025openvlaoft}. Diffusion-based policies generate continuous action sequences through iterative denoising~\cite{chi2025diffusionpolicy,ze2024dp3,liu2025rdt,wen2025diffusionvla}. Flow-matching VLAs, including $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, generate continuous actions by integrating a learned velocity field~\cite{black2025pi0,black2025pi05}.


\subsection{Viewpoint Robustness in Robot Manipulation}

Prior work addresses viewpoint variation using demonstrations collected across views~\cite{ablett2021multiview} or auxiliary views for representation learning~\cite{seo2023multiview}. SPARTN, VISTA, and RoVi-Aug expand viewpoint coverage by synthesizing training observations from alternative camera poses~\cite{zhou2023spartn,tian2025vista,chen2025roviaug}.

Another line of work represents scene geometry explicitly. PerAct operates on voxelized scenes, while RVT projects 3D observations into virtual views~\cite{shridhar2023peract,goyal2023rvt}. 3D-MVP learns geometric representations through multi-view pretraining, whereas Act3D uses a continuous 3D feature field~\cite{qian20253dmvp,gervet2023act3d}. DP3 combines point-cloud observations with diffusion-based action generation~\cite{ze2024dp3}.

\subsection{Privileged Distillation for Weak-View Policies}

Learning with privileged information uses additional information available only during training~\cite{vapnik2015lupi}. Knowledge distillation transfers teacher predictions to a student~\cite{hinton2015distilling}. Privileged distillation trains a student using predictions from a teacher with access to additional information unavailable to the student~\cite{lopezpaz2016generalized}. In embodied control, privileged states and sensors support asymmetric critics and auxiliary models~\cite{pinto2018asymmetric,hu2024scaffolder}. State-based or depth-guided teachers supervise visual policies~\cite{xu2024humanvla,wang2025monolift}.

More directly related to weak-view deployment, Acar et al. distill a multi-camera reinforcement-learning teacher into a single-camera student through action and visual-feature matching, combined with camera-view augmentation~\cite{acar2024multicamera}. However, viewpoint changes alter object visibility and image location, making direct visual-feature matching difficult. ACPD extracts visual cues for each future control step using the teacher's attention weights. The student predicts and uses these cues for action generation.

\section{Action-Conditioned Privileged Distillation}

ACPD uses a frozen privileged-view teacher to supervise a weak-view flow-matching student. ACPD aggregates visual features from each privileged view using attention weights from the teacher's action expert. The aggregation produces a separate cue for each view and future control step. The student predicts these cues from its own observations. A learned gate controls their addition to the student's action representations, which are then decoded into flow velocities for action generation. An action correlation loss (ACL) aligns the relative variation of teacher and student flow-velocity predictions. It complements ground-truth flow-matching supervision. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} illustrates the ACPD framework.

Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the learning problem. Sections~\ref{sec:cue_extraction} and~\ref{sec:cue_fusion} describe cue extraction and student prediction with gated fusion. Sections~\ref{sec:acl} and~\ref{sec:training} present ACL and the training and deployment procedures.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Each sample in the training dataset $\mathcal{D}$ contains a student input $o^s$, a teacher input $o^t$, and a target action chunk $\mathbf{a}$. The two inputs come from the same episode and timestep and share the language instruction and proprioceptive state. They differ only in camera observations. The student input $o^s$ contains a single weak view. The teacher input $o^t$ contains more informative multi-view observations available only during training. The teacher processes $o^t$ to provide distillation targets for the student. At deployment, the student requires only $o^s$.

Both policies use flow matching. The action chunk $\mathbf{a}=(\mathbf{a}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{a}_H)\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d}$ contains the next $H$ actions, each of dimension $d$. For each sample, we draw a flow time $\tau\in[0,1]$ and Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ with the same shape as $\mathbf{a}$. The noisy action is $\mathbf{a}^{\tau}=\tau\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+(1-\tau)\mathbf{a}$. The action expert encodes it as $H$ feature vectors, termed action tokens, one per future control step. The target velocity $\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\mathbf{a}$ points from the action chunk toward the noise.
The student minimizes the mean-squared velocity error:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}}
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\big\|
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}^{\tau},\tau,o^s)-\mathbf{u}
\big\|_2^2
\right].
\label{eq:flow}
\end{equation}
Here, $\theta$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{T}}$ are the trainable student and frozen teacher parameters, respectively. The teacher predicts $\mathbf{v}_{\theta_{\mathrm{T}}}(\mathbf{a}^{\tau},\tau,o^t)$ using the same noisy action and flow time.


\subsection{Action-Conditioned Visual Cue Extraction}
\label{sec:cue_extraction}

ACPD extracts action-conditioned visual cues from a frozen multi-view
teacher as prediction targets for a single-view student. We extract
cues at a selected layer of the teacher's flow-matching action expert.
For each future control step, we aggregate visual features from each
privileged view using the corresponding action token's attention weights.

For attention head $m$, let $\mathbf{Q}^{(m)}$ denote the action-token
queries, and let $\mathbf{K}^{(m)}$ and $\mathbf{V}^{(m)}$ denote the
keys and values of the teacher's token sequence. The attention weights
are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}^{(m)}
=
\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{\mathbf{Q}^{(m)}(\mathbf{K}^{(m)})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}
+\mathbf{B}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $d_h$ is the head dimension and $\mathbf{B}$ is the teacher's
native additive attention bias, including any masking. For each action
token, softmax normalizes the attention weights across the tokens it
can attend to.

For privileged view $r$, let $\mathbf{A}_r^{(m)}$ and
$\mathbf{V}_r^{(m)}$ denote the attention columns and value rows
corresponding to its image-token positions. The contribution of
these positions to the attention output is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Z}_r^{(m)}
=
\mathbf{A}_r^{(m)}\mathbf{V}_r^{(m)}.
\end{equation}
We retain the teacher's original attention weights without renormalizing
them within each view. This provides the student with visual targets
that preserve the teacher's relative emphasis on different views for
each action prediction.

We concatenate the contributions across the $M$ attention heads and
apply the teacher's output projection and time-conditioned scaling:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_r
=
\left[
\operatorname{Concat}_{m=1}^{M}\!\left(\mathbf{Z}_r^{(m)}\right)
\mathbf{W}_O
\right]
\odot\mathbf{s}(\tau).
\label{eq:cue}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbf{W}_O$ denotes the attention output projection and
$\mathbf{s}(\tau)$ denotes the time-conditioned elementwise scale.

The resulting matrix $\mathbf{C}_r\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times D}$
provides one $D$-dimensional prediction target per future control
step. These cues establish supervision at corresponding action
positions, allowing knowledge transfer across camera views
without matching individual image tokens. The student learns
action-related visual cues without reproducing the teacher's
complete hidden representation.

\subsection{Privileged Cue Distillation}
\label{sec:cue_fusion}

Privileged cue distillation (PCD) trains the student to predict
the teacher cues in Section~\ref{sec:cue_extraction}.
Let $\mathbf{H}^s\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times D}$ denote the student's
action-token states at the layer index used for teacher cue
extraction. For each privileged view $r$, a separate
affine predictor $f_r$, shared across action positions, estimates
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_r
=
f_r\!\left(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{H}^s)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{LN}$ denotes layer normalization without
learnable affine parameters. Teacher and student use the same
noisy action chunk $\mathbf{a}^{\tau}$ and flow time $\tau$.

We normalize each view's prediction error by its mean squared
target magnitude and average over $R$ privileged views:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cue}}
=
\frac{1}{R}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\frac{
\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_r,\mathbf{C}_r)
}{
\max\!\left(\operatorname{Mean}(\mathbf{C}_r\odot\mathbf{C}_r),
\varepsilon\right)
}.
\label{eq:cue_loss}
\end{equation}
Both $\operatorname{MSE}$ and $\operatorname{Mean}$ average over
minibatch samples, action positions, and feature dimensions,
with $\varepsilon=10^{-8}$.

Gated cue fusion (GCF) adds the predictions to the student's final action
representations $\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{final}}^s$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^s
=
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{final}}^s
+
\tanh(\gamma)
\sum_{r=1}^{R}
\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\hat{\mathbf{C}}_r\right].
\label{eq:cue_fusion}
\end{equation}
The learnable scalar $\gamma$ is shared across views and action
positions and initialized to zero. The student's existing action
output projection maps $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^s$ to flow-velocity predictions.

The stop-gradient operator $\operatorname{sg}$ blocks flow-matching
and ACL gradients through the predicted cues. The cue loss updates
the predictors and upstream student parameters. The action losses
update the gate and the student's action-generation path. Teacher
supervision thus guides cue prediction, while the gate learns how
strongly to use the cues.

\subsection{Action Correlation Loss}
\label{sec:acl}

Let $\mathbf{v}^{s}$ and $\mathbf{v}^{t}$ denote the student and teacher predictions over the task's action dimensions. Both are computed from the same noisy action $\mathbf{a}^{\tau}$ and flow time $\tau$, conditioned on $o^s$ and $o^t$, respectively. For each sample, we flatten the action horizon and action dimensions as $\mathbf{x}^{s}=\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{v}^{s})$ and $\mathbf{x}^{t}=\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{v}^{t})$. The ACL loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ACL}}
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
1-
\rho\!\left(
\mathbf{x}^{s},
\operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{x}^{t}]
\right)
\right].
\label{eq:acl}
\end{equation}
Here, $\rho$ denotes Pearson correlation.

ACL aligns the relative variation of teacher and student flow-velocity
predictions across control steps and action dimensions, without requiring
pointwise value matching. The flow loss complements ACL by supervising
predicted velocities against the ground-truth target $\mathbf{u}$.

\subsection{Training Objective and Deployment}
\label{sec:training}

The training objective combines flow, PCD, and ACL losses:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}}
+
\lambda_{\text{cue}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cue}}
+
\lambda_{\text{ACL}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ACL}}.
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
With the teacher frozen, we jointly optimize the student, cue predictors, and fusion gate.

The teacher is discarded at deployment. The student uses only $o^s$, with cue prediction and gated fusion unchanged from training.


\section{Experiments}

We evaluate ACPD on four LIBERO suites using synchronized multi-view
data obtained by re-rendering demonstrations from multiple camera
viewpoints. We examine layer selection for privileged-cue distillation
and compare ACPD with single-view fine-tuning across four deployment
views. In the back-view setting, we further compare ACPD with a
multi-view-to-single-view distillation baseline. We also investigate
alternative distillation objectives and conduct incremental ablation studies
to assess the contributions of ACPD's components.

\subsection{Synchronized Multi-View Data Construction}

LIBERO is a language-conditioned robot manipulation benchmark with human-teleoperated demonstrations~\cite{liu2023libero}. We use four suites: LIBERO-Spatial varies the spatial relation identifying the target bowl; LIBERO-Object varies the manipulated object; LIBERO-Goal changes the task goal while keeping the scene fixed; and LIBERO-Long contains 10 long-horizon tasks with diverse interactions and motor skills.

We construct synchronized multi-view data by re-rendering all
demonstrations from six cameras. The setup includes LIBERO's
fixed third-person \textit{agent view},
the robot-mounted \textit{wrist view}, and four additional fixed views:
back, top, left, and right. We manually inspect the additional camera
placements across task scenes to obtain distinct viewing perspectives
while retaining task-relevant visual information. Each timestep is
rendered from all six views, producing synchronized frames. Frames across views
share the same language instruction, proprioceptive state, and target
action chunk.


\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Policy configurations.}
We evaluate four single-view deployment settings using the back,
left, right, and top cameras. Each student receives its designated
camera view, the language instruction, and the robot state.
Single-view supervised fine-tuning (Single-view SFT) optimizes the standard flow-matching
objective. ACPD additionally uses a frozen $\pi_{0.5}$ teacher
fine-tuned with the fixed third-person agent view and
robot-mounted wrist view. These two views provide
privileged visual inputs during distillation, while
each student receives only its designated deployment view.
All students are initialized from the same pretrained
$\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint~\cite{black2025pi05}.
At deployment, ACPD retains its cue predictors and fusion gate,
and requires only the student's designated camera view.

\paragraph{Training settings.}
Students are fine-tuned using LoRA with ranks of 16 for the
vision-language backbone and 32 for the action expert.
Training uses four NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs with fully sharded data
parallelism and a global batch size of 64.
We use AdamW with gradient clipping at a norm of 1.0.
The learning rate follows a linear warmup to $2.5\times10^{-5}$,
followed by cosine decay to $2.5\times10^{-6}$ and a constant
learning rate thereafter. The training seed is 42.
For ACPD, cues are extracted and predicted at action-expert
layer 10, with feature dimension $D=1024$.
We set $\lambda_{\mathrm{cue}}=0.2$ and
$\lambda_{\mathrm{ACL}}=0.5$.

Within each deployment view, Single-view SFT and ACPD share the training
demonstrations, student inputs, pretrained initialization,
and optimization settings.
The configurations used for the distillation baseline and
component studies are specified in their respective subsections.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
All policies predict action chunks of length $H=10$ and
replan after executing five actions.
We evaluate each policy on all four LIBERO suites, using
50 rollouts per task and the same initial-state sequence
and evaluation seed of 7.
Each suite contains 10 tasks, yielding 500 rollouts per suite
and 2,000 rollouts per policy.
We report task success rates, with suite scores averaged
over tasks and the overall score averaged equally over
the four suites.


\subsection{Distillation Layer Selection}
\label{sec:layer_selection}

For layer selection, we use the frozen teacher with
agent and wrist inputs and a frozen back-view student
trained with Single-view SFT. We examine action-expert
layers 6--12 by evaluating how well the student can
predict the teacher's visual cues.
At each layer, separate affine probes predict the agent-view
and wrist-view cues from the student's action representations.
Teacher and student receive the same noisy action chunk and
flow time. We reserve 10\% of demonstration episodes for
validation and average results over three probe initializations.

We evaluate cue prediction using two metrics.
Prediction $R^2$ measures the reduction in mean squared
error relative to predicting the mean teacher cue.
The cosine gap measures how much more similar a prediction
is to the original teacher cue than to a control cue
constructed by shuffling one view's visual features across
samples. Both metrics are averaged over the agent and
wrist views.

\label{sec:ablations}

We examine action correlation supervision, cue distillation,
and gated cue fusion for a back-view student.
All configurations retain the ground-truth flow-matching objective.
Teacher-based variants use the same frozen teacher with agent and
wrist inputs. Table~\ref{tab:controlled_ablations} reports success
on the four suites and their equally weighted mean.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Back-view ablation configurations and task success

distillation; GCF: gated cue fusion. \textit{Avg.} is the mean

\begin{tabular*}{0.95\columnwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Configuration & Spatial $\uparrow$ & Object $\uparrow$

& \underline{64.60} & 24.80 & 53.10 \\

\paragraph{Action correlation supervision.}
Row (a) uses only flow-matching supervision.
Row (b) adds ACL with weight 0.5, without cue supervision or
injection during training or inference.
ACL raises mean success from 57.15\% to 59.75\%, with gains
on all four suites. This improvement establishes a useful
action-level transfer signal before introducing visual-cue learning.

\paragraph{Cue distillation.}
Row (c) adds learned cue distillation (LCD) to ACL.
LCD constructs prediction targets with a trainable
cross-attention selector. At action-expert layers 6 and 12,
the selector uses projected student action representations as
queries and concatenated teacher image and action representations
as keys and values. Three-layer MLPs project these representations
into a shared 1024-dimensional space. A separate three-layer
student predictor estimates the selected cues.
The selector and predictor are optimized jointly using cosine
prediction loss and a variance penalty that discourages constant
cue targets. The combined cue loss has weight 0.2, with relative
weight 0.1 for the variance term; ACL retains weight 0.5.
Selected cues serve only as training targets and are not injected
into action generation. This configuration uses a batch size of
32; the other configurations use 64.

Row (d) combines ACL with PCD, using the normalized
cue-prediction MSE in Equation~\eqref{eq:cue_loss} and
teacher-attention cues extracted at layer 10, without cue fusion
during training or inference.
Unlike LCD, which jointly optimizes the cue selector and
student predictor, PCD defines its targets using the frozen
teacher's own attention computation.
ACL + PCD reaches 58.90\%, compared with 53.10\% for
ACL + LCD, a gain of 5.80 percentage points with higher
success on three suites.
Relative to ACL alone, PCD improves Object and Long but
reduces the four-suite mean by 0.85 percentage points.
Cue prediction alone therefore does not provide an additional
mean success gain over ACL in this setting.

\paragraph{Gated cue fusion.}
Row (e) additionally uses GCF to add predicted cues to the
student's final action representations through a learnable gate,
as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:cue_fusion}.
GCF raises mean success from 58.90\% to 61.05\%, a gain of
2.15 percentage points, with improvements on three suites.
The largest gain is on Spatial, from 65.00\% to 73.20\%;
Object and Long also improve, while Goal decreases from
64.40\% to 63.60\%.
The complete ACPD model exceeds ACL alone by 1.30 percentage
points and Single-view SFT by 3.90 points on average.
These results support coupling privileged-cue prediction
with gated fusion to improve task success.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
We have introduced ACPD for training weak-view VLA policies with privileged camera observations. ACPD combines compact, action-conditioned teacher cues with action-level correlation while retaining single-view inputs at deployment. Experiments across four deployment views show improved weak-view performance, and back-view ablations show higher mean success when cue prediction is combined with gated fusion. These results demonstrate the value of action-conditioned cross-view supervision without requiring additional deployment inputs.
